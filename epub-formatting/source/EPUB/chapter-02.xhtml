II. The Cards

The next morning Mara invited Émile, her aunt’s former apprentice, to sort the cards. He arrived with a paper bag of pears and the cautious expression of someone entering a museum after closing time. Together they made three piles: things that could be found again, things that could only be remembered, and things neither of them understood.

Émile laughed at the table’s seriousness, then stopped when he found a card in his own handwriting. It read, The shop bell rings twice when Mara is brave. He had written it when they were children and had forgotten. Mara did not remember being brave that day, only that she had returned a lost wallet and run away before anyone could thank her.

They began to see the cards as less like evidence and more like invitations. The drawer was not asking them to preserve a perfect history. It was teaching them which ordinary details had once made a day feel inhabited.
